Salmon primary processing — research summary

Purpose
- Collect realistic operating ranges, CAPEX/OPEX, common products and business models for salmon primary processing plants to inform game design and balancing.

- Baader (equipment maker) — example capacity notes: https://www.baader.com/fish/species/salmon

Typical throughput / capacity (recommended game ranges)
- Small/local primary plant: 5–50 tonnes/day (≈1,800–18,000 t/year)
- Medium regional plant: 50–200 tonnes/day (≈18,000–72,000 t/year)
- Large/industrial plant: 200–700 tonnes/day (≈72,000–250,000 t/year)
Notes: many modern filleting lines process 50–150 t/day each; Baader cites plants processing ~700 t/week on two lines (~100 t/day) as realistic.

CAPEX (recommended ranges for purchase/build)
- Small plant (basic gutting, chilling, simple fillet line, cold store): USD 0.5M–2M
- Medium plant (multiple fillet lines, moderate automation, cold storage, packaging): USD 3M–10M
- Large plant (high automation, multiple lines, blast freezing, large cold stores, QC labs): USD 10M–50M+
Drivers: land/site, stainless equipment, automation (vision robots, graders), cold storage, wastewater treatment and regulatory compliance.

OPEX and main cost items (typical composition)
- Raw material (cost of whole fish bought from farmers): single biggest cost for vertically integrated value chain; processors often purchase at farmgate prices. (Not strictly "processing" cost but central to margins.)
- Labor: 15–35% of processing OPEX (higher for manual trimming / value added portioning)
- Utilities (electricity, ice, refrigeration) & maintenance: 10–25%
- Packaging and consumables: 5–15%
- Transport & logistics (inbound/outbound chilled transport): 5–15%
- Waste handling / by-product processing (e.g., heads, guts, roe): variable; can recoup some revenue via by-product sales
- Quality assurance / regulatory compliance / certification costs: modest but rising

Unit OPEX guidance (for balancing)
- Low-efficiency plants: USD 200–500 per tonne processed
- Typical/modern plants: USD 80–250 per tonne processed
These numbers are placeholders for game balancing; actual figures vary strongly by country, automation level, and raw fish price.

Products and yields
- Whole round chilled/frozen (lowest processing margin)
- Headed & gutted (H&G)
- Fillets (skin-on / skin-off)
- Portioning and trimming (higher labour/CAPEX, higher margin)
- Value-added (smoked, marinated, ready-to-eat) — often done in separate facilities
- By-products: fishmeal, oil, roe, pet food raw material — can recover 5–15% of raw weight value
Typical yields (approx): from live/harvested whole fish to fillet: 35–50% yield depending on cut and species.

Margins and payback (game ranges)
- Processor gross margin on processing-only activity: USD 0.1–2.0 per kg of whole fish input (i.e., small per-kg margin unless adding value)
- Value-add (portioning/smoking): can increase margin by USD 2–8 per kg finished product depending on product and market
- Payback periods (CAPEX recovery): 3–8 years realistic for efficient plants with stable throughput; 8+ years if underutilized or in high-capex automated builds.

Business models (real-world examples)
- Integrated farmer→processor→export (vertical integration): Owning farms and processing gives capture of value and risk control
- Contract/toll processing: processor charges fee-per-kg to process farmer-owned fish; low raw-material-price exposure but depends on contracted volumes
- Spot buying and merchant processing: buy fish on spot markets; higher price exposure and working capital needs
- Forward contracts / supply agreements: fixed volumes/prices or exclusive supply to processors or retailers — reduces price risk
- Co-pack / contract manufacturing for brands / third-party label

Common operational risks and events (for game mechanics)
- Fish supply shortage (mortality, disease, low harvest) — reduces throughput, increases raw-material price
- Price shock for raw salmon (market swings) — squeezes margins or creates windfalls
- Equipment failure / line downtime — reduces throughput until repairs
- Labor disputes or seasonal labor shortages — increased labor costs or reduced output
- Regulatory inspection failures / food-safety recall — fines, cleanup costs, reputational hit
- Energy outage / refrigeration failure — product loss risk, urgent CAPEX
- Logistics disruption (port strike, transport shortage)

Realistic game mechanics suggestions
- Build vs Invest decision: build your own plant (higher CAPEX, full control, higher margins) vs invest in existing plant (lower CAPEX, minority returns, less operational control)
- Capacity upgrades: incremental (add another fillet line) vs step-change (new cold store / blast freezer) with different CAPEX and time-to-complete
- Throughput utilization: plant has nominal capacity; throughput scales profits non-linearly (economies of scale). Example: profits per tonne increase when utilization >60%.
- Chart of accounts (for simulation): raw material purchases, processing labor, utilities, packaging, transport, maintenance, depreciation, fixed overheads, by-product revenue
- Contract types to model: exclusive supply (guaranteed volume, lower price volatility), tolling (fixed fee/kg), spot (full price exposure), forward-fixed (volume + price hedging)
- Risk events as cards or random events with mitigation options (insurance purchase, redundant equipment, buffer cold storage, alternative buyers)
- Productivity upgrades: automation tech reduces labor per tonne but increases CAPEX and may increase maintenance/energy

Numeric recommendations for balancing (summary)
- Plant sizes (t/day): small 5–50; medium 50–200; large 200–700
- CAPEX (USD): small 0.5M–2M; medium 3M–10M; large 10M–50M+
- OPEX (USD/tonne): typical 80–250; low-efficiency 200–500
- Yield to fillet: 35–50%
- Payback: 3–8 years (optimistic) or 8+ if underutilized

Notes, caveats and next steps
- These are synthesized, high-level ranges drawn from industry equipment vendors, seafood handbooks, and regional studies. Actual values differ by country (Norway vs Chile vs Canada), scale, labor costs, and market prices.
- For a game, use the ranges above and tune to gameplay. Consider region modifiers (e.g., Norway: higher CAPEX but cheaper logistics to EU; Chile: lower labor cost, different risks).
